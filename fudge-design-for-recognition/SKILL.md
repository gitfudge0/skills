# Design for recognition

Make the next useful thing apparent from the interface itself. Own the design outcome through the draft, inspection, and revision; scale the work to the size of the request.

## Work from the person's task

1. Establish who uses the interface and what they must find, understand, decide, and do. Check the surrounding product for language, patterns, and constraints before changing them.
2. Map the information and attention path. Decide what needs attention first, what supports the decision, what belongs together, and what can wait. For one control, this may be its label, state, consequence, and feedback. For a workflow, trace the path across steps.
3. Choose placement, order, hierarchy, recognizable cues, and interaction feedback to make that path clear. Use alignment, proximity, and grouping to express relationships. Add dividers, borders, or cards when they make groups easier to scan or distinguish meanings.
4. Produce a usable draft when creation is in scope. Use realistic content and the states that affect the task, such as empty, pending, error, selected, or completed states. For a usability review, point to the exact place where a person may lose the path and propose a concrete correction.
5. Inspect the rendered UI at relevant sizes and follow a realistic task through the available interaction. Fix unclear labels, weak hierarchy, misplaced actions, missing feedback, and dead ends that the inspection exposes. Repeat only as needed to make the key path work.

If a meaningful uncertainty remains that the user's interaction can settle, invite them to try one focused task on the working draft, then revise from what happens. Keep design decisions and the revision work with the agent; an unwired description is not a substitute for a usable draft.

The work is done when the intended person can tell where to look, what the information means, what choice to make, how to act, and what happened after acting. Judge this against the actual task, not a preferred component style.
